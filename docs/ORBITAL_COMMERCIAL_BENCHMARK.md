# Orbital Direct — commercial benchmark

What kinds of change at a company plausibly create demand for Orbital Direct,
and how to judge whether a given change actually does.

This is a reasoning framework, not a keyword list. A keyword list would match
"distribution centre" and miss "we are consolidating three sites into one",
which is the same commercial event described differently. The test is always:

```
WHAT CHANGED?
  → WHY DOES IT MATTER?
    → WHAT COMMERCIAL CONSEQUENCE COULD IT CREATE?
      → WHY IS ORBITAL RELEVANT?
        → WHAT SHOULD A SALESPERSON DO?
```

A change that cannot complete that chain is not an opportunity, however
newsworthy it is.

## What Orbital actually sells

Freight forwarding (air, road, sea) · same-day courier and UK haulage ·
warehousing, e-commerce fulfilment and FBA prep · customs clearance and
documentation · dangerous-goods shipping · export packing and crating · cargo
insurance.

Two things follow. Orbital is an **SME forwarder** — it competes on
responsiveness, specialist handling and proximity, not on beating a global 4PL
on rate at volume. And its differentiators are **specialist**: dangerous goods,
export packing, customs complexity, urgent same-day. A change that creates
ordinary pallet volume is a weaker fit than one that creates awkward,
regulated or urgent freight.

## Change families

Each family is scored on the same question: does this change alter the
*physical movement of goods*, and in a way Orbital can serve?

### Strong — the change directly alters goods movement

| Family | Why it matters | Orbital relevance |
|---|---|---|
| Distribution centre opening | Distribution footprint changes; inbound and outbound flows are re-planned | Haulage into and out of the new site; overflow at peak |
| Warehouse move or relocation | Every logistics arrangement is re-procured at once | The rare moment an incumbent is genuinely displaceable |
| Warehouse expansion / outgrown capacity | Volume has exceeded the current footprint | Overflow storage, 3PL capacity |
| Manufacturing expansion | More output to move, inbound materials to feed it | Inbound and outbound freight |
| Production relocation or reshoring | Routes and customs treatment change entirely | New lanes, new documentation |
| New geographic market entry | A lane that did not exist has to be created | Freight, customs, packing for an unfamiliar destination |
| Export or import growth | Existing lanes carry materially more | Rate review, consolidation, groupage economics |
| Major contract win | Delivery capacity must scale to serve it | Capacity, project freight |
| Project or tender activity | Materials and plant must reach a site, often overseas | Project cargo, oversized freight, export documentation |
| Acquisition or divestment | Networks are merged or separated; supplier lists reopen | Consolidation of flows, re-tendering |

### Moderate — the change implies goods movement indirectly

| Family | Why it matters | Caveat |
|---|---|---|
| Investment or funding | Capital is committed to something physical | Only if the spend is on capacity, not software or R&D |
| New product launch | New SKUs need distribution | Weak unless volume or destination changes |
| Property expansion | More space usually means more throughput | Confirm it is operational space, not offices |
| Transport / warehouse / logistics hiring | The clearest leading indicator of volume change | Strong signal, but a hiring feature is not yet built |
| Supplier or 3PL change | The incumbent is being reconsidered | Rarely announced; usually inferred |
| Consolidation | Fewer sites, longer internal legs, re-planned distribution | Cuts both ways — could reduce total spend |
| Restructuring | Cost pressure reopens supplier arrangements | See below — this is the subtle one |

### Restructuring and contraction — not automatically irrelevant

A company cutting cost is not buying growth, but it may well be **reviewing
suppliers**. Consolidating two sites into one changes internal transport.
Exiting a market strands inventory that must be moved. Contraction is a valid
signal family where the consequence is specific and evidenced.

What it must not become: treating every redundancy announcement as a
cost-reduction sales opening. The consequence has to be named and evidenced,
not assumed.

### Weak or excluded

- **Awards and recognition** — confirm performance already achieved. Not a
  change. Useful only as ICP confirmation.
- **Leadership appointments** — unless the appointee owns logistics.
- **Financial results** — unless they evidence a volume change.
- **Enterprise-scale logistics projects** — Aldi, Tesco and similar run their
  own networks and are unreachable for an SME forwarder.
- **Third-party logistics providers** — competitors, not prospects.
- **Service businesses with no physical goods** — no freight to win.

## The disqualifying questions

Before any change is called an opportunity:

1. **Does it move goods?** If the change is purely digital, financial or
   organisational with no physical consequence, stop.
2. **Is the volume within Orbital's reach?** Too small is not worth a
   salesperson's time; too large belongs to a global 4PL.
3. **Is it current?** A change that completed two years ago has already been
   arranged by someone.
4. **Is the consequence specific, or is it "they may need logistics"?** The
   latter is the failure mode this benchmark exists to prevent.
5. **Who handles it now?** Almost every established exporter has an incumbent.
   That does not kill the opportunity, but it changes the sale from filling a
   gap to displacing a supplier, and the pitch must reflect it.

## Reasoning depth

A strong opportunity is explicit about all three levels, and honest that the
third is a question rather than a finding.

- **Direct signal** — what a source states. *"Opened a 67,000 sq ft
  distribution centre on 31 July."*
- **Second-order consequence** — what that plausibly changes. *"Inbound and
  outbound flows through a new main hub are re-planned; peak volume may exceed
  a building sized for the average."*
- **Third-order sales question** — what we do not know and must ask. *"Who
  handles overflow today, what changed from the previous arrangement, and when
  does the new site hit its first peak?"*

The engine should produce the third-order question. It must never present an
answer to it.

## Two-axis assessment

Evidence quality and commercial value are independent, and conflating them is
how a well-sourced irrelevance gets sold as an opportunity.

|  | High commercial value | Low commercial value |
|---|---|---|
| **High evidence** | Ideal — act now | Interesting, not worth sales time |
| **Low evidence** | Research priority — verify before contact | Reject |

## Gold labels

- **A — Strong opportunity.** Specific, current, evidenced change with a clear
  Orbital-relevant consequence. Worth a call this week.
- **B — Potential opportunity.** Real change, plausible consequence, but with a
  material unknown — incumbent, volume, or timing.
- **C — Weak or irrelevant.** ICP may fit, but the change does not create an
  Orbital-relevant consequence.
- **D — False positive.** Reported as an opportunity when the reasoning does
  not hold.
- **E — No actionable change.** Researched; nothing current found.
- **F — Insufficient evidence.** A consequence is plausible but nothing
  evidences it.
